When it's a real emergency

The assistant does intake, never judgment. Here's exactly what happens when a call sounds urgent:

  1. Deterministic triggers, not AI guesswork. No heat, no cool, burst pipe, gas smell — urgent keywords trip the escalation path every time, by rule.
  2. Straight transfer to your on-call tech. The caller is handed off warm, with a private briefing of what the assistant already collected.
  3. A human calls back within 10 minutes if your on-call line is busy — the customer hears a promise with a clock on it, not "we'll pass along your message."

Common questions

“Will customers hate talking to an AI?”

It identifies itself as an assistant right away, sounds natural — a US-trained, plain-English voice — and most callers still prefer it to voicemail. If a caller wants a person, it hands them warmly to one. Listen to the recordings — the proof's right there.

“What about real emergencies?”

Triage is conservative by design: the assistant does intake only, never judgment. Anything that sounds urgent — no heat, no cool, burst pipe, gas smell — triggers an immediate transfer to your on-call tech, and if the line's busy, a human calls the customer back within 10 minutes. No AI sits on a real emergency.

“We already have an answering service.”

They take messages — you still start over on every call. Bullpen books the job onto your calendar while the customer's still on the line, and there's no per-minute meter running.

“A local human service only costs $200/mo.”

For message-taking — and the per-minute meter means real volume runs ~$1,170/mo anyway. The question isn't the fee, it's whether the job gets booked before the customer calls the next name on Google.

“What if it messes up?”

It starts on your overflow and after-hours calls only — nothing you're already handling gets touched. You review every recording in the first few weeks, so you see exactly what it says before it earns more of your call volume.
